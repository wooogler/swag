SCORE · LLM-as-a-judge reliability

How steady is the judge?

A test–retest audit of the 5-level rating that powers SCORE's starter-set subtypes — the same student request, scored eight times, to see how often the verdict holds.

01

How far do the eight runs swing?

Every one of the 520 (query × subtype) judgments was rated 8 times. This is the spread between the highest and lowest of those 8 verdicts, on the 4-step ordinal scale — the share that never moved, moved one step, two, or flipped end-to-end.

02

The instability map

Each square is one (query × subtype) judgment. Darker = the 8 runs disagreed more. A red outline marks the cells where the run-to-run wobble actually crossed the clearly_in line that decides assignment — the flips that change behavior. Note how instability is sparse and clustered, not spread evenly.

03

Which subtypes are shaky?

Mean run-to-run agreement per starter-set subtype (share of the 8 runs landing on the modal verdict, averaged over the 20 queries). Narrow, lexically-cued subtypes are rock-solid; broad or overlapping ones wobble. A ▸ flags subtypes whose clearly_in decision flipped on ≥10% of queries.

04

Ambiguity, or noise?

For each of the 28 decision-flip cells we asked three fresh, independent judges (blind to the production runs) to each pick the single most defensible rating. Where the independent panel also splits, the query is genuinely ambiguous and the instability is expected — the fix is intent design, not the model. Where the panel concentrates but production waffled, that is model noise.

    How this was measured

    The judge is the exact production path — rateMessageIntents → callModel — with the real starter-set definitions, prior-context strategy, strict 4-level schema, and default reasoning effort. SCORE's models are reasoning models that accept no temperature, top_p, or seed, so this measures the judge's inherent run-to-run nondeterminism — the floor you actually get in production, not a temperature artifact.
